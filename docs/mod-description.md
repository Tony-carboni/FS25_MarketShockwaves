# Market Shockwaves — store description

Copy for ModHub / KingMods / Steam Workshop listings and for forum posts. Five
versions below: a one-liner, a short blurb, a short description for forum posts,
a plain-text description for ModHub, and the full page copy. Current for version
0.9.2.0. A separate guide for video makers is in `docs/creator-guide.md`.

**Written to preserve the surprise.** The whole design rests on the player not
knowing how big an event is or how long it will run, so this copy names no
magnitudes, no durations, no severity odds and no ripple timings. It says what
the mod *does* and what it *feels like*, and leaves the numbers to be discovered.
The roster is listed in full — knowing an ethanol boom exists spoils nothing
about how one behaves.

Droughts, embargoes and bumper harvests that move market prices for months and

## Short description for forum posts (~130 words)

**Market Shockwaves** makes selling a decision again.

Market events move prices for months at a time: droughts, bumper harvests,
embargoes, export booms, hard winters, a pandemic. Each one builds, holds and
corrects along its own curve, and the shock travels down the production chain,
so wheat moves first, then flour, then bread. You are told what happened. You
are never told how big it gets or how long it lasts.

New in 0.9.2.0: **forecasts**. Analysts tell you months ahead what they expect
for next year's wheat, oilseeds, corn, potatoes, timber or winter feed, and how
sure they are. They are timed by your map's own crop calendar, so you can still
choose what to plant. Usually they are right. Sometimes everyone plants for the
boom and it becomes a glut.

Nineteen events, six forecasts, its own page in the ESC menu, works on any map.
Singleplayer.

## Full page copy

### Market Shockwaves

**The prices in Farming Simulator never surprise you.** They drift inside a
predictable seasonal band, you learn the good months for each crop, and after a
season or two selling stops being a decision and becomes a lookup.

Market Shockwaves changes that. It adds a world of market events — droughts,
embargoes, gluts, export booms, bad winters — that move prices for months at a
time and force you to read the market instead of memorising it.

---

### What it feels like in play

You get a message. Not a number — a market report:

> **Market Alert: Severe Drought**
> A severe, prolonged drought has struck the region's field crops — wheat,
> barley, oats, maize, sorghum, canola, sunflower and soybean — and prices are
> spiking fast. Straw is scarce as well, with thin harvests leaving little
> behind. The shortage will hold high for an extended spell, likely up to the
> next harvest, and ripple deep through the production chain as processed goods
> grow scarce in turn.

From that point on, the wheat in your silo is worth more every week. **How much
more, and for how long, you are never told.** You can see today's price. You
cannot see tomorrow's.

So you have a real decision. Sell into the rise and take a good profit — or hold,
and back your read that this one runs deeper. Some events are a pleasant few
weeks. Others reshape a whole year. **They open the same way**, with the same
kind of report, and the only way to tell them apart is to watch the market and
trust your judgement.

Sometimes you will sell early and watch it climb without you. Sometimes you will
hold too long and watch the harvest take it all back. That is the game.

---

### Prices move along a curve, not a step

An event is not a flat modifier that switches on and off. Every one has a shape:

- **A build**, as the market digests the news.
- **A peak** — and it may come sooner than you expect.
- **A long plateau**, drifting rather than dropping.
- **A correction** when the new harvest lands: quick, but not a cliff, and it
  reaches every affected product at once. Nothing is left stranded high after the
  market has moved on.

A little daily noise rides on top, so prices tick and wander the way a real
market does instead of sitting on a rail. You will not spot the shape from the
inside. You only ever see today.

---

### Shocks travel down the production chain

This is what makes the economy feel joined up. When grain spikes, the shock works
its way downstream — but not immediately:

```
WHEAT   →   FLOUR   →   BREAD   →   CAKE
```

Each layer follows the one above it after a delay, and each passes on less than
it received. Your bakery does not spike the day the drought is announced. It
spikes later, once expensive flour has worked its way through.

**How deep a shock travels depends on how bad it is.** A mild event barely leaves
the field. A severe one reaches the top of the chain and stays there a while. So
a serious shock genuinely changes what is worth producing this year, while a
small one is just a good week to be holding wheat.

Chains are wired so nothing is hit twice by the same event — wheat drives flour,
so barley doesn't drive it as well.

---

### Events stack

Events don't queue up politely. A grain drought and an input shock can run at
once, and their effects compound — expensive fertiliser on top of scarce grain is
a harder year than either alone. When two shocks push the same product the same
way, the second adds less than it would alone, so a pile-up bites without
running away. A single event never overlaps itself, and a configurable ceiling
stops an unlucky run from piling up without end.

---

### The roster — nineteen events across seven categories

Each category can be switched off independently.

**Crops**
- **Grain Drought** — wheat, barley, oats, canola, maize, sorghum, sunflower and
  soybean tighten up. Works through to flour, bread, cake, cereal, the oils and
  straw. *Late winter into early autumn, worst through the growing season.*
- **Bumper Grain Harvest** — the mirror. Too much of everything, and prices sag.
  *Around and after harvest.*
- **Root & Vegetable Drought** — potato, sugarbeet, beetroot, carrot, parsnip,
  green bean, pea and spinach. Reaches chips, sugar, chocolate, the preserves and
  the soup cans. *Spring through autumn.*
- **Root & Vegetable Glut** — the mirror. *Late summer into autumn.*

**Greenhouse**
- **Greenhouse Pest Outbreak** — lettuce, tomato, strawberry, spring onion, napa
  cabbage, chilli and garlic. *Any time of year, but pests like the warm months.*
- **Greenhouse Bounty** — the mirror: protected crops come in heavy.

**Trade**
- **Dairy Export Embargo** — cheese, butter, goat cheese, buffalo mozzarella and
  the bottled milks lose their export market.
- **Dairy Export Boom** — the mirror: overseas demand surges.
- **Mediterranean Export Embargo** — olive oil, grape juice, raisins and
  tomatoes.
- **Mediterranean Export Boom** — the mirror.
- **Southeast Asian Trade Disruption** — the region's export routes close, and
  the rice trade closes with them. Rice, long grain rice and enoki mushrooms
  tighten; oyster mushrooms are bid up as the substitute. Works right through the
  milled, packed and prepared rice goods. *Whenever it lands.*
- **Favourable Monsoon** — the mirror. A generous monsoon gives the region a huge
  rice crop and cheap freight carries it everywhere. *Autumn into the new year,
  once the harvest is in.*

  *The embargoes and the disruption fall evenly across the calendar — they hit
  when they hit, not when the weather says so. The monsoon is the exception, and
  it keeps a farmer's schedule.*

**Biofuel**
- **Ethanol Boom** — biofuel demand pulls at maize, sugarbeet, sugarcane and
  soybean.
- **Ethanol Glut** — the mirror: the biofuel market sours.

**Winter**
- **Harsh Winter** — diesel, electricity and methane get expensive. This one
  hits your running costs, not your sales. *The cold months.*

**Supplies**
- **Natural Disaster** — a relief and rebuilding effort lifts construction
  materials: prefab walls, roof plates, cement, bricks, and the timber chain
  behind them. Blankets and warm clothing are shipped in too, so clothing pulls
  fabric and raw cotton up with it. *Any time, though the storm season is worse.*
- **Input Price Shock** — fertiliser, liquid fertiliser, lime and seed all jump
  at once, right when you need them. Manure, slurry and digestate firm up too as
  growers reach for substitutes — small consolation in an expensive spring.
  *Winter into spring.*

**Consumer**
- **Pandemic** — households stay home and stock the cupboard. Soups, canned peas,
  jarred beans and preserved vegetables sell as fast as they can be made, and
  canneries pay more for the vegetables that go in them. With restaurants quiet,
  fresh greenhouse produce, cheese, olive oil and grape juice lose ground.
  *Whenever it lands.*
- **Trendy Lifestyle** — the mirror. Fresh and local becomes the fashion:
  greenhouse produce, cheese, olive oil and grape juice climb, dairies and
  presses pay more for milk, olives and grapes, and nobody wants a tin.
  *Whenever it lands.*

---

### Fair, but not soft

- **Good and bad events fire about as often as each other.** For every embargo
  there is an export boom; for every drought, a bumper harvest.
- **When the market turns against you, it turns more gently than when it goes
  your way.** Deliberate. A bad year should hurt, not end you.
- **Nothing is hidden from you unfairly.** Every event announces itself, names
  the products it touches, and hints at how far it expects to spread. What you
  are not given is the ending.

---

### Never the same twice

Every event rolls its own severity, and then rolls its own numbers inside it.
Magnitude, duration, where the peak falls, how sharply it climbs, how far it
ripples — all drawn fresh, every time.

Two severe droughts, ten in-game years apart, will not behave the same. There is
no curve to learn and no table to memorise, and that is the whole point. Play
long enough and you will still be caught out.

---

### Forecasts

An event is a surprise: you react to it. A forecast is the opposite. It gives
you time to change your plans, and no guarantee.

About once a year, suppliers, analysts or forecasters tell you what they expect,
months ahead, and how sure they are:

> **Mills Eye Next Year's Grain**
> Flour mills and cereal makers report that their reserves are running thin, and
> analysts expect them to compete hard for wheat and oats by next harvest. If
> that holds, both will fetch noticeably more than they do today. Experts largely
> agree. Wheat goes in this autumn, and oats follow in spring.

**It arrives while you can still act.** A forecast is timed by your map's own
crop calendar: the message comes before sowing opens, and the outcome lands when
that crop is harvested. On a map with different seasons, the forecasts move with
them.

**Then you wait.** Prices do nothing for months. Just before the verdict they
edge the way the analysts called it, which tells you the answer is close but
not what it is. Then the verdict lands, and from that moment it is a real market
event that lasts until the next harvest and runs down the production chain like
any other.

**The experts are usually right, and say how sure they are.** "The majority
seems to agree" is a weaker promise than "experts are unanimous", and it means
exactly what it says. Sometimes it hits harder or softer than predicted. And
sometimes everyone plants for the boom, and the boom becomes a glut:

> **Grain Glut**
> Even experts can be wrong, it seems. Everyone planted for the grain boom, and
> harvests are fatter than ever. Silos are overflowing, and mills are naming
> their price.

**Six forecasts, each in two directions:**

- **Milling grains** — wheat and oats, with flour, bread, cereal, straw and hay
  behind them.
- **Vegetable oil** — canola and sunflower, and their oils.
- **Biofuel policy vote** — corn and soybean.
- **Food processors** — potatoes, sugar beet and sugarcane, with chips and sugar.
- **A sawmill opening or closing** — timber one way; boards, planks, beams and
  wood chips the other.
- **Winter feed outlook** — a hard winter lifts silage, hay, grass and straw, and
  energy with them; a long grazing season does the reverse.

Planting, holding or selling on a forecast is a gamble; how much you stake is
up to you.

### The Market Shockwaves page

The mod has its own page in the ESC menu, with four tabs:

- **Market** — what is in the news right now, and every product whose price is
  moving today. Select a product to see what is behind it.
- **Forecasts** — what has been announced and is still undecided.
- **History** — every message the mod has shown you, with its date, so nothing is
  lost if you clicked a report away too quickly.
- **Settings** — below.

None of it tells you how an event or a forecast will end.

### Settings

On the **Market Shockwaves** page in the ESC menu, under its **Settings** tab.
Each row explains itself in the description panel. The same page's **Market**
tab shows what is in the news and which prices are moving today.

| Setting | What it does |
|---|---|
| Enable Market Shockwaves | Master switch. Running events finish naturally rather than being cut off. |
| Events per year (average) | How busy the market is. Set it to zero for a quiet world. |
| Minimum time between events | Guarantees calm stretches between shocks. |
| Maximum events at once | Ceiling on how much can stack. |
| Event intensity | Scales every price swing, up or down. |
| Event duration | Scales how long events run. |
| Supply chain effects | Caps how far ripples climb — raw crops only, or all the way up. |
| Show price markers | Adds the current change to affected commodity names. |
| Forecasts | Switches forecasts on or off. One already announced still gets its verdict. |
| Forecasts per year | How many forecasts arrive in an average year. |
| Event categories | Seven independent toggles. |

Want a wilder market? Turn intensity and frequency up. Want the occasional
shock and long calm spells in between? Turn them down. Want only weather events
and no geopolitics? Switch the trade category off.

**Events per year is honoured regardless of your season length.** The mod rolls
once per in-game day and scales the odds to your days-per-month, so a one-day
month and a twelve-day month both give you about the same number of events a
year. Set your calendar however you like.

---

### Price markers

With markers on, affected goods carry their current change in the name — say
`Wheat [+14%]` — in the price list, the shop and storage displays. One glance
tells you where the market is right now. It still won't tell you where it's
going. Turn it off if you'd rather read the raw prices yourself.

---

### Good to know

- **Works on any map.** The mod doesn't edit `fillTypes.xml` and ships no map
  data. It wraps the game's price lookup and multiplies the result, so any map's
  own prices — and any crops it adds — keep working underneath.
- **Plays nicely with other economy mods.** Prices are wrapped rather than
  replaced, and stored economy data is never modified, so other price mods stack
  with this one instead of fighting it.
- **Saves with your game.** Active events, their curves and your settings live in
  the savegame and resume exactly where they left off.
- **Singleplayer.** Multiplayer sync is planned but not in yet.
- **No performance cost.** Prices are worked out once an in-game day, not per
  frame.

---

### Feedback

Balance feedback is genuinely useful — especially whether events feel too
frequent, too tame or too brutal at your season length. Bug reports are best with
your `log.txt`.

## Notes for whoever pastes this

- **Don't add numbers back in.** No magnitudes, durations, severity odds or
  ripple timings anywhere player-facing — screenshots, changelog, forum replies.
  Someone who knows a severe event's ceiling has lost the decision the mod is
  built around.
- ModHub's description field is plain text: use the ready-made plain-text
  version above, not the full page copy.
- The harsh winter is described as hitting running costs. Whether the mod moves
  the price PAID for diesel and inputs, and not only the selling price, has not
  been confirmed in game; check before leaning on that line in a listing.
- KingMods and Steam accept BBCode: `[b]…[/b]` for headings, `[list]` for
  bullets. Steam supports `[table]`; KingMods doesn't.
- Good screenshots: the event message dialog, and the price list with markers
  showing. Both convey the idea without giving figures away.
- Don't caption a price-graph screenshot as showing event history — the graph
  plots elapsed months only, so an event mid-run shows partially.
- Add a version and changelog section before listing.
